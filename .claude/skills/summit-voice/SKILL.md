# Summit voice

Adapted from the [Google developer documentation style guide](https://developers.google.com/style).
Google's guide was written for API documentation. This skill keeps its clarity
machinery, which is what fixes copy that reads as artificial, and carves out the
places where a trade summit legitimately needs to persuade.

## The one rule

**Write the sentence a person would say to a buyer across a table.** Then delete
the words they wouldn't have said.

If a sentence could appear on any of two hundred other event websites without
changing a word, it is not doing any work. Cut it.

---

## Part 1 — Hard rules (bind on every page, no exceptions)

These come straight from Google and they are non-negotiable.

### 1.1 Third person to describe, second person to instruct

**This overrides Google.** Google mandates second person throughout. Client
decision of 23 August 2026: constant *you* reads as instructional and slightly
pushy on a trade-summit site.

**Describe how things work in the third person**, naming the actor:

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| Delegates create a profile saying what they sell or want to buy. | You create a profile saying what you sell or want to buy. |
| Exhibitors choose a stand from the plan and hold it. | You choose a stand from the plan and hold it. |
| Journalists apply through media accreditation. | You apply through media accreditation. |

**Use second person only for direct instructions, calls to action and form
labels**, where the reader is being asked to act right now:

- *Register your interest*
- *Apply at etakenya.go.ke*
- *Tell us which level interests you*

Never *we*, *our* or *us* for the reader's actions. First-person plural is for
the Secretariat's own actions, used sparingly. In Draft A the Secretariat is
named as "the Secretariat", not "we".

### 1.2 Active voice, and name who acts

Passive voice is the single largest cause of the "artificial" feeling. It hides
the actor, which makes copy sound like it was assembled rather than written.

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| The Secretariat confirms your stand within two working days. | Stand confirmations will be issued within two working days. |
| Kenyan producers meet Chinese buyers. | Connections are facilitated between producers and buyers. |

Passive is allowed in two cases only: when the object genuinely matters more
than the actor (*The programme is published in phases*), or when naming the
actor would be gratuitous.

### 1.3 Present tense

No future tense for how the site or the summit works. No hypothetical *would*.

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| Registration opens in October. | Registration will be opening in October. |
| If you register as an exhibitor, the portal shows the floor plan. | If you were to register as an exhibitor, the portal would show you the floor plan. |

### 1.4 Conditions before instructions

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| To exhibit, register as an exhibitor first. | Register as an exhibitor first, if you want to exhibit. |

### 1.5 Sentence case for every heading

Headings, page titles, buttons, nav labels, table headers. Sentence case. Proper
nouns keep their capitals.

*Book your stand* — not *Book Your Stand*.

### 1.6 Descriptive link text

The link text must say where it goes, read on its own, out of context.

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| See the [exhibitor pack](#). | For the exhibitor pack, [click here](#). |
| Read the [stand sizes and rates](#). | [Read more](#) |

### 1.7 Write for a global audience

Most readers of this site read English as a second or third language. Chinese
delegates read the English edition too, because machine translation of an
industry term is often worse than the original.

- No idioms, no metaphors, no cultural references. **"Brewing partnerships" is
  a metaphor.** So is *landscape*, *journey*, *unlock*, *ecosystem*, *bridge*.
- One idea per sentence. Target 15–20 words, hard ceiling 25.
- Prefer the shorter word: *use* over *utilise*, *help* over *facilitate*,
  *about* over *approximately*, *start* over *commence*.
- **No serial (Oxford) commas.** *Black, green and orthodox tea* — not *black,
  green, and orthodox tea*. This overrides Google, and matches British usage.
- **No em dashes. None.** Use a full stop, a comma, or a colon. If a sentence
  needs an em dash it is two sentences.
- Unambiguous dates (**21–23 April 2027**, never 21/04/27). The en dash in a
  date range is a range marker, not a dash, and stays.
- Expand an acronym on first use per page: *Kenya International Convention
  Centre (KICC)*.

### 1.8 Accessible by construction

- Every image gets alt text describing what it shows, not what it is. *Two
  buyers examining leaf samples at a tasting table* — not *Photo of tea*.
- Never use *simply*, *just*, *easy*, *quickly*, or *obviously* about something
  the reader has to do. It tells a struggling reader the fault is theirs.
- Never *see below* or *above*. Link to the section.
- No exclamation marks. None.

## Part 2 — Kenya carve-outs (these override Google)

Google mandates American spelling. **This site does not follow that**, and the
override is deliberate: the client is Kenyan, the Secretariat is Kenyan, and
Kenya writes British English. American spelling on a Nairobi summit site reads
as outsourced.

- **British spelling throughout.** organisation, authorise, specialise,
  centre, licence (noun), programme, catalogue, travelled.
- **Programme**, never *program*. It is the word in the client brief and in the
  navigation.
- Currency as **KES 25,000** and **USD 200**. Give both wherever a price appears.
- Metric only. Stand sizes in metres: **3m × 3m**.
- 24-hour clock for session times: **09:00**, not 9am.
- Times carry the zone on any page a Chinese delegate reads: **09:00 EAT**.

## Part 3 — The persuasion carve-out

Google's guide has no concept of selling anything. This site has to sell stands
and sponsorships. So:

**Clarity rules in Part 1 bind everywhere and are never suspended.** But on the
homepage, `/sponsorship/`, `/exhibition/` and `/registration/`, each section may
carry **one** sentence whose job is to persuade rather than inform.

That sentence still obeys Part 1. It is not an exemption from plain English —
it is permission to make an argument. The way you make an argument in this
voice is with a **specific fact**, not an adjective.

| Persuades | Does not persuade |
|---|---|
| Kenya produces more black tea for export than any other country, and sells most of it unbranded. | Kenya is a world-leading tea powerhouse with immense untapped potential. |
| 146 stands. Last year's equivalent expo sold out in six weeks. | Secure your place at this landmark, unmissable event. |

If you cannot find a fact, you do not have an argument, and the sentence should
not exist. **Never invent one to fill the slot** — see Part 6.

## Part 3a — Confidence

Client decision, 23 August 2026: **the site reads confident everywhere.**

An honest gap and a negative sentence are not the same thing. State the gap
neutrally. Never apologise for it, never dwell on it, and never phrase a fact
in terms of what is missing or what does not work.

| Write | Not |
|---|---|
| Speakers are announced in phases. | Speakers are not yet confirmed. |
| Registration takes two minutes. | Registration takes two minutes and does not commit you to attending. |
| Request the partnership pack. | Ask and the Secretariat sends you the pack as soon as it exists. |
| Market access, standards and pricing. | …and where the two currently fail to meet. |
| Registration fees: **[TBA]** | Fees have not been set yet. |

Rules:

- **Never open a sentence with a negative.** No *There is no…*, *Nothing is…*,
  *Not yet…*, *We cannot…*, *We don't have…*.
- **`[TBA]` stands alone.** It is a neutral marker, not an admission. It needs
  no sentence around it explaining or excusing it.
- **Do not raise a question the site cannot answer.** If a fact is unconfirmed
  and no reader has asked, the sentence does not exist. Deleting is more
  confident than hedging, and it is still honest.
- **Never describe what something is not**, unless the reader would otherwise
  get it wrong. The two exceptions that stay, because both prevent a real
  mistake: the expo is in a tent on the grounds rather than inside KICC, and
  the Westlands address is the Secretariat office rather than the venue.

**The boundary this does not cross:** confidence is about framing, never about
claims. Nothing here permits inventing a fact, implying a confirmation that does
not exist, or dropping a caveat that protects the reader. "The layout is subject
to change" stays. Visa guidance stays. FACTS.md still binds. See Part 6.

## Part 4 — Length budgets

The client's benchmark comparison is correct: this site is roughly three times
the length of comparable tea-summit sites. `worldteacoffeeexpo.com` runs about
520 words on its homepage. This site ran 1,569.

| Page type | Target (visible body words, excluding header and footer) |
|---|---|
| Homepage | 400–500 |
| Primary pages (about, programme, exhibition, registration, sponsorship) | 300–450 |
| Secondary pages (venue, travel, media, B2B, contact) | 250–350 |
| FAQ | 600 total, and nothing that another page already answers |
| Legal pages | No budget. Completeness beats brevity. |
| News post | 400–700 |

Section rules:

- No section longer than 3 paragraphs.
- No paragraph longer than 4 sentences.
- If a page needs a scroll on desktop to reach its first call to action, it is
  too long above the fold.
- A bulleted list of 4 short items beats a paragraph containing the same 4
  items. Prose is for argument; lists are for inventory.

## Part 5 — Banned constructions

Each of these is a phrase found in draft or live copy, with its replacement.

**Dead openers.** Delete and start with the noun.

- *The summit is designed to…* → *The summit…*
- *This platform serves as…* → *It…*
- *We are committed to…* → say what you actually do
- *It is worth noting that…* → delete entirely
- *In today's rapidly evolving…* → delete entirely

**Abstract nouns that name nothing.** Replace with the concrete thing.

| Banned | Use |
|---|---|
| solutions | the equipment, the software, the service — name it |
| opportunities | the specific opportunity |
| stakeholders | buyers, exporters, regulators, investors — name them |
| ecosystem, landscape, space | the industry, the market, or nothing |
| synergies, leverage, unlock, empower | a verb that means something |
| engagement, connectivity, cooperation | who is doing what with whom |
| world-class, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, premier, landmark | a fact, or nothing |
| journey, immersive, curated, bespoke | delete |
| robust, seamless, holistic, dynamic, vibrant | delete |
| foster, facilitate, drive, deliver, spearhead | help, run, make, do |
| delve into, dive deep, at the heart of | delete |

**Hedges.** They read as evasion and they are usually there because a fact is
missing. If the fact is missing, say *To be announced* instead.

- *may include*, *aims to*, *seeks to*, *is intended to*, *among others*,
  *and more*, *various*, *a range of*, *approximately*, *potentially*

**Filler intensifiers.** Delete on sight.

- *very*, *really*, *truly*, *highly*, *significantly*, *incredibly*,
  *extremely*, *particularly*, *especially*, *quite*

**Three-item adjective strings.** Pick one, or none.

- *innovative, sustainable and transformative* → delete all three

**AI syntax giveaways.** These are structural rather than lexical, which makes
them harder to spot and more damaging. Each one reads as machine-assembled.

- **Two-beat antithesis.** *A delegate meets the people they already planned to
  meet. A stand gets you the buyer who was walking past.* Two sentences of
  matched shape doing one job. Write the one sentence that carries the point.
- **Not just X, it's Y.** Any form of it. Delete the first half.
- **Tailing negation.** *…and hold it, no back-and-forth.* *…in two minutes, no
  commitment.* A clipped fragment bolted to the end for punch.
- **Rule of three.** Three nouns, three adjectives, three clauses. Two is
  natural. Three is a tell. Four is a list and should be formatted as one.
- **Inline-header bullets.** *- **Trade:** Connect producers with buyers.* Either
  write the sentence or write the list. Not both in one line.
- **The restated-heading opener.** A heading followed by a sentence that says
  the heading again before the real content starts.
- **Explaining the structure instead of using it.** *Here is what is confirmed.*
  *Three things follow.* State them.
- **Synonym cycling.** *The summit… the event… the gathering… the forum.* Pick
  the word and repeat it. Repetition reads as confidence; variation reads as
  padding.

**Rule of thumb:** if the sentence still means the same thing after you delete a
word, the word was never doing anything. If a paragraph still means the same
thing after you delete its first sentence, that sentence was scaffolding.

## Part 6 — Facts

`website_content/FACTS.md` §1–§3 bind all copy and this skill does not relax
them.

- **Never invent a fact to improve a sentence.** Not a number, not a date, not a
  claim about scale, not a superlative, not a partner name.
- Where a fact is not confirmed, write **To be announced**. Do not hedge around
  it, do not write *details coming soon*, do not pad the gap with adjectives.
  The bare phrase is more credible than the padding.
- Only these are confirmed and may be stated: dates 21–23 April 2027; venue
  KICC, Nairobi, with the expo in a tent on the grounds; organiser Orbitline
  Events & Ushers Ltd; Tea Board of Kenya as partner; the contact details in
  `src/_data/summit.js`.
- Superlatives (*first*, *largest*, *only*, *premier*) require written client
  confirmation. Without it they do not appear.

## Part 7 — Check before you ship

Run this on every page you write.

1. Read it aloud. Anywhere you stumble, the sentence is wrong.
2. Delete the first sentence of each section. Does the section still work? If
   so, it stays deleted.
3. Search the draft for every word in Part 5. Each hit is a defect.
4. Count words against the Part 4 budget. Over budget is a defect, not a
   preference.
5. Check every heading is sentence case and every link text reads on its own.
6. Check no sentence exceeds 25 words.
6a. Search for `—`. Every hit is a defect (Part 1.7).
6b. Search for `, and`, `, or`. Every serial comma is a defect (Part 1.7).
6c. Count `you` and `your`. If either appears outside an instruction, a call to
    action or a form label, rewrite it in the third person (Part 1.1).
6d. Search for `not`, `no `, `never`, `yet`, `cannot`, `unconfirmed`. Each hit
    is a candidate defect under Part 3a.
6e. Read the structure, not the words. Any two-beat antithesis, rule of three
    or tailing negation is a defect (Part 5).
7. Check every claim against Part 6. Anything unconfirmed becomes
   *To be announced*.
8. Would a Chinese buyer reading English as a third language parse this on one
   pass? If not, shorten it.

## Part 8 — Chinese edition

This skill governs the English source only. The `/zh/` edition is generated by
`scripts/translate.js` and **`src/_data/i18n/zh/*.json` is never hand-edited**.

Shorter, more concrete English produces markedly better machine translation,
which is a second reason the budgets in Part 4 matter. Metaphors translate
worst of all — another reason Part 1.7 bans them outright.
